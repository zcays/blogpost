Trump wants YOU off his Lawn

A first-generation student examines immigration, political power, and the stories America tells about who belongs.

Written by Ivan Chao

July 27, 2026
Begin reading ↓
Page02

Essay

Introduction

Year by year, immigrants immigrate to the USA in search of hope and opportunity. My family is one of those immigrants. My dad actually immigrated twice. The first time was to escape his home country, Myanmar, during the military takeover of the government. He immigrated to Macau, where he met my mom, who had just escaped from the communist rule in China to seek asylum. Their lives were tough; they worked hard shifts to even afford a small, rundown apartment that would always have leaks. In hopes of having a better life, they then immigrated to the US, and that was where I was born.

Many immigrants share similar stories to mine, coming from hard lives and seeking opportunity in the US. This TED Talk from Tan Le tells her own story of immigration.

Hi, my name is Ivan, and I come from a first-generation immigrant family. Luckily, my family and I did end up having a better life, enough for me to even head to college. I have been a student of LATI10 at the University of California, San Diego. In that class, we tackle issues like hegemony, racism, and oppression specifically in the Global South. Over the course of analyzing different materials, such as how Guatemala was portrayed by the USA in the past and the events during the Chilean dictatorship conducted by Pinochet. All of which have their own cases of oppression. This then leads to my focus question of how Trump’s discussing on how criminalization of immigrants is represented and how they are affected. After discussing examples of criminalization, I will introduce British Jamaican theorist Stuart Hall and discuss how his framework from his 1996 “West and the Rest: Discourse and Power” essay, helps us understand the actions of the administration.

Trump’s Take on US Immigrants

Since Trump’s presidency, even in his first term in office, he proudly stood on being hard against immigration. This was even shown by this quote from Trump himself:

“I am going to create a new special deportation task force, focused on identifying and removing quickly the most dangerous criminal illegal immigrants in America” (PBS NewsHour)

This clearly shows his distaste for illegal immigrants and labels all illegal immigrants as people who are dangerous, when it is completely false. This is proven from an historical analysis of incarceration (being confined in a prison or jail) by two groups, US-born vs. first-generation immigrants.

This data shows that, historically, on average, US-born citizens actually commit more crime than first-generation immigrants, which completely disproves his claims about immigrants being dangerous when what we should be worrying about are US-born citizens being criminals.

According to Merriam-Webster, hegemony is described as “strong influence or authority over others” (Merriam-Webster Dictionary). The quote from Trump closely ties to this definition, as Trump wanted to create a political task force to have control over the immigrant community. Without a doubt, these immigrants faces racial hegemony from Trump that may also encourage other individuals to attack these communities.

Who He Blames?

In the most straightforward manner, he directly blames other countries in the Global South for immigration issues. In a 2024 speech, Trump targeted South American migration patterns by claiming that "Venezuela crime is down 72%" due to the government sending criminals to the United States (Factcheck.org, Trump’s Claim). Racism is shown here as he targets and attacks Venezuelans for immigrating to the USA because they are all “criminals” burdening the country.

Analytical statement:

Time and time again, immigrants in the USA get labeled as people of criminal origin and intent by people of power. Stuart Hall’s essay perfectly encompasses everything that is happening. Again he was Jamaican-born sociologist and political activist who created new ways of analyzing culture + media + society and wrote the essay 1996 “West and the Rest: Discourse and Power”. In Stuart Hall’s 1996 essay, “The West and the Rest,” he explains how the West made itself look modern and civilized while making other countries look undeveloped and dangerous. Hall calls this discourse, which is how language and ideas can create an implied truth about people. This shows how the West used representation to gain power and justify colonialism.
